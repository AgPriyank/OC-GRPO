Off-Context GRPO: Learning to Reason on Hard Problems using Privileged Information

When training has information that inference does not, one importance correction keeps the objective honest.

RLVR is only as good as its rollouts

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards depends entirely on the quality of the rollouts it trains on. GRPO learns from within-group reward variance: some trajectories in a group succeed, others fail, and the successful ones are reinforced relative to the failures. For a given base model, hard problems tend to produce groups where every trajectory fails. Rewards are then uniformly zero, the group-relative advantages are zero, and the gradient signal is exactly zero, no matter how long training continues. We call this the learning cliff.

We construct the sharpest version of the setting: from MATH Levels 3-5 we keep the 595 problems on which Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct fails all 64 sampled attempts, so vanilla GRPO receives no learning signal at all by construction.

Privileged information improves rollout quality

A natural workaround is to inject additional information into the training prompt so that correct continuations become reachable. Prepending a prefix of the reference solution lifts the expected verifier reward on this pool from 0.09 to 0.79. The cliff is breakable.

But those rollouts are off-context

Here is the subtle part. Tokens produced under the richer context follow a different distribution than the policy under the original prompt. They are off-context. Guided methods sample rollouts under the guided prompt and then compute the update as if the sampling and evaluation distributions matched, carrying the guided prompt in both the numerator and the denominator of the policy ratio.

The consequence is that training makes the model better at a shifted objective: the one that solves the problem with privileged information available. That is not the objective served at inference, where no guidance exists, so performance on the original objective is limited. Masking the guidance tokens out of the loss does not fix it either, because masking changes which tokens receive gradient, not what they condition on.

OC-GRPO corrects the bias

OC-GRPO targets settings with a natural information asymmetry: training time has privileged information that improves rollout quality, test time does not. It is a one-line change to a GRPO implementation. We correct the bias from off-context rollouts with a designed per-token importance correction in the log-probability ratio of the GRPO objective, replacing the standard ratio with one whose numerator conditions on the original prompt and whose denominator conditions on the guided prompt that actually produced the token:

ρoct(θ) = πθ(yt | x, y<t) πθold(yt | g(x), y<t)

x is the original prompt, g(x) the guided prompt. With no guidance, g(x) = x and the update is exactly vanilla GRPO.

The corrected estimator is unbiased for the original, unguided objective. There is no reward shaping, no entropy control, and no auxiliary SFT loss. The overhead is a single no-grad forward pass per step, about 5.5% of step time at 7B, and nothing at inference. The variance of the correction scales with the length of the guidance rather than the rollout, which gives a clean operating rule: use the shortest guidance that breaks the cliff.

Credit in proportion to unguided ability

The same correction turns out to do something more interesting than fixing the objective. We prove it induces behavior-aware credit assignment: the model receives credit in proportion to its propensity to solve the hard problem without the guidance. Averaged over successful guided rollouts the correction factor equals p(x)/q(x) < 1, and over failed ones (1−p(x))/(1−q(x)) > 1, where p and q are the success rates under the original and guided prompts.

A success that leaned on the guidance is discounted in proportion to how much it leaned. A failure that persists despite the guidance draws an amplified penalty. The update reinforces solving rather than hint-following, with no additional design choices.

Results

On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (LoRA, veRL, 4 epochs, 16 rollouts per prompt, 3 seeds), evaluated on AIME (1983-2026), Gaokao2023 and OmniMath: OC-GRPO reaches 31.1 average Pass@1 against 27.3 for vanilla GRPO, a 13.7% relative gain, ahead of every guided baseline under an identical protocol. Pass@16 rises as well, from 49.6 to 51.4 on the held-out benchmarks and from 7.0 to 14.8 on the hard training pool, so the gains do not come from collapsing the model's sampling diversity.

The pattern sharpens at smaller scale. Guided methods without the correction rely on the hope that improvement under the guided prompt transfers to the unguided one. At 7B a large model absorbs much of the mismatch; at 3B and 1.5B it does not. Uncorrected guided baselines fall below vanilla GRPO, by as much as 7.2%, while OC-GRPO retains gains of +7.2% and +10.2%. The correction matters most exactly where capacity is scarcest.

Takeaway

Privileged information helps roll out richer trajectories. OC-GRPO provides the robust optimization objective that corrects the bias that privileged information introduces. The recipe applies however the guidance is generated, whether solution prefixes, tiered hints, self-generated scaffolds, or a teacher model, and extends naturally to agentic RLVR, where the privileged context is a tool result or an oracle subgoal.
